Recovering Terrain Height from a Single Image

Single-view height estimation and 3D terrain reconstruction from optical imagery

Knowing how high the ground, the buildings and the trees stand is the starting point for flood modelling, infrastructure planning, disaster response and environmental monitoring. Today that information is almost always measured — by airborne LiDAR, by pairs of overlapping satellite images, or by radar — and each of those routes needs dedicated acquisition. DepthWizard asks a different question: how much of that elevation can be inferred from one ordinary optical image, the most abundant kind of Earth-observation data there is?

Our answer is a three-stage pipeline. A foundation monocular depth model, Depth Anything V2, is fine-tuned on the GAMUS remote-sensing benchmark so that it reads top-down imagery correctly. Its relative height predictions are then converted to metres by a scale calibration fitted separately for each land-cover class against SRTM 30 m reference elevation, and every pixel receives a confidence estimate from test-time augmentation. The result is a digital surface model that can be explored as a 3D terrain in the browser.

The system accepts georeferenced imagery (GeoTIFF, producing an absolute DSM in metres) and plain images without coordinates (producing a relative DSM). It runs entirely on local compute and is a research prototype: fine-tuning is still in progress, so every figure on this site is reported as the latest verified result and is updated as new runs complete.

The Problem

Why recovering height from one image is hard

A single photograph contains no direct measurement of height. Closing that gap takes a learned model, an external reference, and care about resolution.

Elevation is expensive to measure

High-resolution elevation models are produced by instruments that must be pointed at the ground on purpose. Airborne LiDAR is highly accurate, but each survey needs an aircraft, a flight plan, favourable weather and substantial processing. Stereo photogrammetry recovers height from two or more overlapping images taken from different angles, so it depends on repeat acquisitions and is easily defeated by cloud cover. Interferometric radar (InSAR) sees through cloud, but its measurements degrade with surface decorrelation and atmospheric delay, and steep terrain introduces layover and shadow distortions. For a specific site at a specific time, any of these can mean waiting weeks or having no data at all.

Images are abundant, but they are flat

Optical satellite and aerial imagery, by contrast, is archived at enormous scale and is often available within hours. It is rarely used for elevation because a single image records brightness and colour, not distance. A roof and a car park can have the same colour and very different heights.

The mathematical obstacle

Recovering height from one image is an ill-posed inverse problem: infinitely many three-dimensional scenes project onto exactly the same two-dimensional picture. A neural network can still learn strong priors about how the world tends to look — that shadows fall beside tall objects, that roofs have edges, that canopy has a characteristic texture — and so predict a plausible relative height for every pixel. What it cannot recover from the picture alone is the absolute scale and offset that turn those relative values into metres. That final step needs information from outside the image.

The nadir domain gap

Modern depth foundation models are trained overwhelmingly on ground-level, perspective photographs, where vanishing lines, horizon position and object size give strong depth cues. A satellite looks straight down. Those cues vanish, and the model must rely on cast shadows, roof-edge geometry and texture instead. The pixel scale also differs: a model trained on tiles at roughly 0.33 m per pixel behaves unpredictably when handed an image with a very different ground-sample distance. Adapting a foundation model to this geometry, and controlling for resolution, is a research problem in its own right.

Objectives

What this project sets out to do

Six objectives, each paired with the evidence that will show whether it was met.

01

Adapt a depth foundation model to nadir imagery

Fine-tune Depth Anything V2 on GAMUS (11,507 paired RGB and height tiles) so that predicted height reflects roofs, canopy and ground in top-down imagery rather than the perspective-scene priors it was pretrained with.
Measured by: per-land-cover-class mean absolute error against the zero-shot backbone on held-out tiles.
02

Recover absolute height in metres

For georeferenced input, anchor the relative prediction to SRTM 30 m elevation, fitting the scale and offset per land-cover class. For input without coordinates, deliver a self-consistent relative DSM and say so.
Measured by: error of per-class versus single global calibration in a controlled multi-bias test, and behaviour on real tiles.
03

Stay reliable when resolution changes

Normalise ground-sample distance so that imagery at arbitrary resolution reaches the model at a pixel scale close to what it was trained on, instead of silently producing structurally meaningless output.
Measured by: whether output structure remains meaningful on Sentinel-2 imagery outside the training resolution (qualitative).
04

Report confidence, not just height

Produce a per-pixel confidence map from test-time augmentation, so that a user can see where the prediction is trustworthy and where it is not.
Measured by: whether the variance signal is structured and concentrates at height discontinuities rather than being flat.
05

Deliver an interactive 3D explorer

Render the calibrated surface as a browser-based terrain model with elevation, solid and imagery modes and both orbit and first-person navigation, running on the user’s own machine with no hosted service.
Measured by: a complete input-to-3D run on local compute using only the packaged outputs.
06

Report the evidence honestly

Keep failed experiments in the record, mark pending results as pending, and make every number on the site traceable to a documented run.
Measured by: the full iteration history and the technical archive in Documentation.
Proposed Solution

The DepthWizard approach

Decouple shape from scale: let a fine-tuned foundation model predict structure, and let an external reference and land-cover labels supply the metres.

Predict relative height from one image

What it does. A Depth Anything V2 backbone (a DINOv2 Vision Transformer encoder with a DPT decoder) takes the image, after ground-sample-distance normalisation, and predicts a relative height value for every pixel. Only the last transformer blocks and the decoder are fine-tuned; the earlier blocks keep their general visual features.

Why this choice. Training a height network from scratch would need far more labelled elevation data than exists for remote sensing. A pretrained depth model already encodes strong priors about edges, surfaces and layout; fine-tuning on GAMUS teaches it what those priors mean when viewed from above. A composite loss (scale-invariant, multi-scale gradient, and a penalty focused on buildings and trees) keeps edges sharp and stops the easy flat-ground pixels from dominating training.

Output. A relative height map, edge-refined with a guided filter using the RGB image as guide.

Turn relative height into metres

What it does. The relative height map is anchored to a real elevation reference. For georeferenced input the pipeline retrieves SRTM 30 m for the same area, resamples it to the prediction grid, and fits an affine correction a·h + b — separately for each land-cover class — by least squares.

Why this choice. One correction for the whole scene assumes buildings, trees and ground all deviate from the reference in the same way. Our evaluation shows they do not, and prior work on joint height and semantic estimation reaches the same conclusion. Classes with too few pixels to fit reliably fall back to the global fit.

Output. An absolute DSM in metres for georeferenced input; a relative DSM otherwise.

Show where the prediction is trustworthy

What it does. The same image is passed through the model eight times under fixed perturbations — the identity, horizontal flip, vertical flip, both flips, and four brightness or gamma shifts. Each output is mapped back to the original orientation and the per-pixel variance across the eight is the uncertainty.

Why this choice. Monte Carlo Dropout is the usual tool, and we tried it first. It failed here: the backbone’s dropout probabilities are all zero, so repeated passes were numerically identical and the confidence map was a flat constant. Perturbing the input instead gives a signal that is real and interpretable.

Output. A confidence texture aligned with the height map, lowest at building and canopy edges.

Explore the result in three dimensions

What it does. The calibrated surface is edge-refined, its building footprints are flattened into planar roofs using the land-cover mask, and the result is packaged as a 16-bit heightmap with a confidence texture and the source imagery. A Three.js viewer decodes the heightmap into a mesh.

Why this choice. A browser viewer needs no GIS software, which matters for the planners and analysts this is intended for. Sixteen bits give 65,536 height levels instead of 256, avoiding visible terracing on gentle terrain.

Output. An interactive terrain with elevation-colour, solid and imagery render modes, orbit and first-person flythrough navigation.
